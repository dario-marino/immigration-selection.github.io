What this tool shows

Every immigration system makes a choice about who gets in. This tool lets you try out different rules and see which immigrants would qualify under each one.

It uses data on more than one million immigrants who live in the United States today, drawn from the Census Bureau’s American Community Survey. We split each country’s immigrants into profiles based on sex, education (five levels, from less than high school to a graduate degree), and age when they arrived in the US (before 30, or at 30 or older). For example, one profile is “Italian women with a bachelor’s degree who arrived before age 30.” Each country has up to 20 profiles.

There are two kinds of screens. Home-country screens (GDP per capita and PISA test scores) describe the country itself. US-outcome screens (income, institutionalization, and English) describe how people from that country are actually doing after arriving in the United States, not conditions back home. When you set a bar, the map shades each country by how many of its profiles clear it, and clicking a country shows exactly which profiles pass.

Why these measures

  • Income, education, and age at arrival. Earned income is the most direct measure of what an immigrant adds to the economy and pays in taxes, which is why public benefits are excluded from it here. Manhattan Institute research finds that immigrants with college or graduate degrees, particularly those who arrive young, generate large net fiscal surpluses, while those without college degrees are net costs. See Daniel Di Martino, “The Lifetime Fiscal Impact of Immigrants” (2024) and “The Fiscal Impact of Immigration (2025 Update)”.
  • Institutionalization. Because the Census counts people living in prisons and other institutions, its microdata are a standard way to estimate incarceration by country of birth. See Michelangelo Landgrave and Alex Nowrasteh, “Illegal Immigrant Incarceration Rates, 2010–2024” (Cato Institute, 2026).
  • English proficiency. English skill is strongly tied to immigrant earnings and integration. See Barry Chiswick and Paul Miller, “The Endogeneity between Language and Earnings: International Analyses,” Journal of Labor Economics (1995).
  • GDP per capita. A country’s level of development reflects long-lived institutions and conventions that migrants carry with them. See Diego Comin, William Easterly, and Erick Gong, “Was the Wealth of Nations Determined in 1000 BC?” American Economic Journal: Macroeconomics (2010), and Garett Jones, The Culture Transplant (2022).
  • PISA scores. National test scores of 15-year-olds are among the strongest predictors of long-run economic growth. See Eric Hanushek and Ludger Woessmann, “The Role of Cognitive Skills in Economic Development,” Journal of Economic Literature (2008).

How it’s built

Data. Immigrant profiles are computed from the 2020–2024 American Community Survey 5-year sample (IPUMS USA), using person weights, for immigrants aged 18 and over. Each profile is defined by sex, education (less than high school; high school diploma; associate’s degree or some college; bachelor’s degree; graduate degree), and age at arrival in the US (0–29 or 30+, computed as year of immigration minus birth year). Profiles built from fewer than 30 sampled records are marked unreliable and never count toward a country’s total.

Income is total personal income minus Social Security, public assistance, and Supplemental Security Income (IPUMS INCTOT net of INCSS, INCWELFR, INCSUPP), converted to 2026 dollars using the CPI-U.

Institutionalization is the number of people per 100,000 living in institutional group quarters, restricted to ages 60 and under so that nursing-home care does not dominate. The public ACS file does not say which type of institution someone is in, so prisons, psychiatric hospitals, and other institutional care are combined. Treat this as a proxy, not a crime rate.

English proficiency is the share of a profile who report speaking English “very well” or who speak only English.

GDP per capita is PPP-adjusted, most recent year available. PISA is the average of each country’s math, reading, and science mean scores from its latest cycle (2022 for most). OECD does not publish a combined score, so this average is our convention. Hong Kong, Macao, and Chinese Taipei are reported separately; mainland China has no national score, and several large origin countries (including India) do not take part. Those countries show as no-data, not as zero.

US-born reference values come from the same 2020–2024 ACS file, restricted to adults (18+) born in the 50 states and DC, using identical definitions. The US GDP and PISA figures come from the same sources as the other countries.
